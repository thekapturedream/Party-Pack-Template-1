# Where the leads actually are

Written 9 September 2026, after establishing that there is no list of individual
email addresses to be found lawfully or otherwise. That was the wrong thing to
look for.

## The reframe

For a £22 consumer printable with no audience, you do not find individuals. You
either get found by people already searching, or you go to **organisations that
already have the nine-to-twelve-year-olds assembled in a room**.

The second one is the better answer, for three reasons.

**It is legally different.** UK PECR restricts unsolicited marketing email to
"individual subscribers". Schools, libraries, councils, charities and limited
companies are *corporate* subscribers, and marketing email to them is permitted,
subject to UK GDPR: a legitimate-interests basis, honest identification of the
sender, and a working opt-out in every message. Sending a relevant offer to a
school's published office address is not the same act as mailing a parent whose
address you scraped.

Note the boundary carefully. **Sole traders and unincorporated partnerships count
as individual subscribers.** A self-employed children's entertainer is therefore
off-limits for unsolicited email, while a limited-company party venue is not.
Check the legal form before adding anyone to the list.

**The economics are better.** A school buying one licence for a year group is
worth more than a parent buying one party, and it does not need Etsy's 6.5%.

**The volume needed is tiny.** The commercial gate is ten purchases. Ten schools
is a fortnight of careful emails, not a marketing campaign.

## Who to contact, ranked

The licence already permits classroom, library and club use, printed as often as
you like. That was written into the kit before any of this, and it is the thing
that makes all of the below legitimate rather than a stretch.

**1. School libraries and primary schools, Years 5 and 6.** The exact age. The
buying moments are end of term, the last week before a holiday, wet break, and
Year 6 transition activities. Contact the school office address published on the
website, addressed to the librarian or the Year 6 lead by name where the site
gives one.

**2. Public libraries.** Holiday activity programmes are planned a term ahead and
run on almost no budget, which is precisely the shape of a printable. Library
services buy centrally, so one email to a county service can reach thirty
branches. Highest leverage per email on this list.

**3. Scout, Guide, Cub and Brownie groups.** Weekly programmes that constantly
need an hour of indoor activity, and district commissioners circulate what works.
Group emails are published on group websites.

**4. Home-education networks and co-ops.** Buy for a group, meet weekly, and talk
to each other constantly. Reach them through their organisers rather than by
posting into their forums.

**5. Holiday clubs and after-school clubs.** The largest audiences, but read the
licence first: it forbids running the kit as a paid event. A holiday club charging
admission needs a licence the product does not currently sell. See below.

**6. Party venues and entertainers.** Only the incorporated ones, per the PECR
boundary above. Lower priority: they want a service to resell, and the licence
forbids resale.

## How to find them, without buying a list

All of this is published information, gathered by hand rather than scraped.

- **Schools:** GOV.UK's Get Information About Schools gives every school in
  England with phase, size and local authority. Filter to primary, then take the
  office address from each school's own website.
- **Libraries:** each county council publishes its library service contact and
  its holiday programme page.
- **Scouts and Guiding:** both organisations publish group finders by postcode.
- **Home education:** organisers run public groups; approach the organiser.

Start local. A Zambian-timezone business selling to UK schools is fine, but the
first ten replies will come from places with some connection to you, and warm
beats cold every time.

## What to send

Two templates are in `store/outreach.md`. They are short on purpose. The offer is
not "buy our kit"; it is "here is a complete free puzzle, use it this term".

Rules that make the difference between outreach and spam:

- One organisation, one email, written to that organisation. No mail merge that
  shows.
- Lead with the free puzzle, not the product. A librarian can use it on Thursday.
- Say who you are and where you are, in the first line.
- Every email carries a plain opt-out sentence. Honour it immediately.
- One follow-up, a fortnight later, and then never again.
- Keep a record of who was contacted and who opted out. That record is the
  accountability requirement under UK GDPR, and it stops the second round
  embarrassing you.

## The licence gap worth closing

The kit forbids running it as a paid event. That excludes holiday clubs, paid
workshops and party businesses, which is a meaningful slice of the list above.

Wix Pay Links is installed on the site, and the Payment Links API can charge for a
**custom line item** without creating a catalogue product — which sidesteps the
Catalog V1 block on creating digital products entirely. So a commercial or
multi-site licence can be sold today at a higher price, with the files delivered
by hand. At the volumes this would run at, manual delivery is not a problem.

Do not build that until at least one organisation asks. It is a note, not a task.

## What success looks like

Ten purchases is the gate from the brief. Against this list:

- 50 well-written emails is a realistic fortnight.
- A 10% reply rate on genuinely relevant B2B outreach is normal.
- Some of those replies are a librarian saying "send me the sample", which is a
  consented address and the beginning of the list that does not exist yet.

That is slower than buying a list and enormously more durable, because every
address on it was given rather than taken.
