Most homemade escape rooms fail the same way. The puzzles are all the same puzzle. There are six envelopes, and all six contain a code to crack, so the one child who is good at codes solves the whole party while the other seven watch.

The fix is not harder puzzles. It is different ones.

## The rule: six puzzles, six kinds of thinking

Design the set so that no single child can dominate it. That means each puzzle should reward a different ability, and the abilities should be ones children actually vary in.

These six work, and they cover the range:

**A substitution cipher.** Letters swapped by a fixed rule, with a decoder strip so it is decipherable rather than guessable. Rewards patience and method. This is the one that looks hardest and is in fact the most reliable to solve.

**A physical search.** An answer that is written somewhere in the room, or formed by holding two printed sheets against a window. Rewards the child who gets out of their chair. Every group has one, and this is their moment.

**Arithmetic with a real purpose.** Not a sum for the sake of a sum. Four times on four clock faces, and the difference between them is the number you need. Rewards the child who is quick with numbers without turning the party into homework.

**A logic grid.** Five statements, four suspects, one of them is lying. Rewards the child who is methodical rather than fast, which is a different child from the arithmetic one.

**A cross-reference.** An answer that only appears when you put two separate sheets side by side. Rewards whoever kept the papers organised, and it teaches the group to stop discarding things.

**Sequencing.** Events, or objects, that have to be put in the right order. Rewards the child who notices what everyone else is skipping past.

That spread is the whole trick. Six children can each own one puzzle.

## Make two of them self-checking

The single biggest problem when you run this at home is that you become the answer key. Eight children queue up to ask if they are right, and the momentum dies each time.

Build at least two puzzles so that two independent routes reach the same answer. If the clock arithmetic and the logic grid both produce 1847, the team knows they are right without asking. You get to stay out of it, which is the difference between hosting and refereeing.

## Write the hints before the party, not during it

Improvised hints are always too generous. Under pressure, with a child looking at you, you will give away the answer.

Write three levels for every puzzle, in advance:

1. A nudge that points at the right sheet.
2. A hint that names the method.
3. The answer.

Then hand them out on cards rather than saying them aloud, so only the team that is stuck gets helped. A stuck team should never be stuck for more than a minute; that is the actual quality bar.

## Make the paper look like paper, not like a party game

This is where homemade kits usually give themselves away. Comic-sans instructions on coloured card read as school worksheet, and nine-to-twelve-year-olds are merciless about it.

What works: a typewriter face, a rubber stamp, a filing tab, a document that looks photocopied. High contrast, black on white, nothing rounded and nothing jolly. You are making a case file, and the aesthetic is doing real work — it is the thing that tells the child this is worth taking seriously.

It also prints for pennies, which is the other reason to avoid full-bleed colour.
